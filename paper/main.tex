\begin{abstract}
Differentiable digital signal processing is widely used for sound
matching and timbre transfer because it combines efficient synthesis with
interpretable controls and permits label-free analysis by synthesis. However,
the end-to-end recovery of fundamental frequency remains difficult.
Conventional waveform losses and pointwise spectral distances compare
corresponding samples or time--frequency bins without representing the
direction or extent of a frequency displacement. For oscillatory signals,
their loss surfaces therefore contain local minima and broad regions whose
gradients provide little indication of whether a candidate frequency should
increase or decrease. Consequently, these systems frequently obtain
fundamental frequency from an external pitch estimator; event timing may
similarly be fixed by an onset detector or event annotation. The reconstruction
objective is then unable to revise these variables when different values would
better explain the target sound.

Independently, existing sound-matching systems commonly occupy one of two
temporal regimes: linear time-invariant processors with static parameters, or
time-varying models with densely sampled control trajectories. Dense control
is appropriate for continuously driven instruments, but many plucked and
struck instruments are more naturally described as sparse musical events. We
therefore formulate monophonic phrase matching as the direct recovery of a
sequence of plucks. Each pluck is represented by a single onset coordinate
$t_i$ and a fundamental frequency $f_{0,i}$ held for that event, rather than
by framewise pitch and onset trajectories.

Spectral optimal transport has been proposed to improve frequency estimation
by measuring the displacement of energy along the frequency axis instead of
comparing fixed bins, while joint time--frequency optimal transport extends
this principle across both axes. We evaluate these approaches using a
differentiable digital waveguide. We choose this renderer because a compact
feedback-delay structure produces the rich, decaying harmonic response of a
plucked string while exposing pitch through a physically meaningful
propagation delay, analogous to string length. Its excitation, filtering,
delay, and feedback components are also shared with broader families of
source--filter synthesizers and artificial reverberators, making it a useful
test case beyond this particular instrument. We introduce a bidirectional
cumulative-energy loss that compares normalised cumulative spectral-power
surfaces in both directions of both time and frequency. This loss is the only
evaluated loss whose descent direction points towards the target at every
sampled point in the single-pluck landscape. Across 6000 direct
analysis-by-synthesis fits, as phrases grow from one to eight plucks, classical
waveform and spectral losses and framewise spectral optimal transport
frequently collapse to poor multi-event solutions, whereas the cumulative
loss recovers pluck pitches and onset times more reliably than joint
time--frequency optimal transport.

Separately, we show that gradient direction within the waveguide depends on
how continuous pluck placement and fractional delay are implemented. Fourier
pluck placement combined with causal Thiran fractional-delay sections
produces stronger and more consistently target-directed gradients than a
frequency-sampled delay-line realisation. Together, these findings support
end-to-end differentiable synthesis wherever pitch, event timing, or
delay-line length must be optimized directly from audio.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Differentiable digital signal processing (\ddsp) places interpretable audio
processors inside an automatic-differentiation graph, enabling
analysis-by-synthesis systems in which synthesizer controls are inferred by
minimising an audio reconstruction objective
\cite{engel2020ddsp,caspe2022ddx7}. Existing systems commonly adopt one of two
temporal control regimes. At one extreme, a sound is represented by a single
global parameter vector, as is appropriate for a fixed or approximately LTI
processor. At the other, synthesizer parameters are predicted as dense
frame-rate trajectories, as in DDSP resynthesis and differentiable FM
synthesis. Dense control is suitable for continuously driven instruments and
evolving modulation, but may be unnecessarily high-dimensional when the
principal musical changes occur at a small number of discrete instants.

This contrasts with the symbolic representations commonly used in Music
Information Retrieval (MIR). Sheet music, MIDI, and automatic music
transcription describe music primarily through note events, identified by
quantities such as onset, pitch, duration, and velocity
\cite{hawthorne2018onsets}. Consequently, audio-domain DDSP systems and
symbolic music systems often describe the same performance at markedly
different temporal resolutions. For plucked and struck instruments, a natural
intermediate representation is a sparse set of excitation events
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}=\{(t_i,f_{0,i})\}_{i=1}^{C},
\end{equation}
where each event is a pluck with onset $t_i$ and fundamental frequency
$f_{0,i}$. This representation concerns the occurrence and pitch of the
plucks; it does not require every other synthesizer control to remain constant
between them. It therefore moves differentiable sound matching closer to a
musically meaningful, note-centred representation while retaining direct
audio-domain supervision.

Related work has also sought compact control representations between global
parameters and unconstrained framewise trajectories. Mitcheltree et al., for
example, represent synthesizer modulation through low-dimensional constrained
curves and piecewise B\'ezier splines \cite{mitcheltree2025modulation}. Their
objective is continuous modulation discovery rather than discrete note-event
recovery, but the underlying philosophy is similar: the parameterisation
should reflect the structure with which musical sounds are produced. More
directly, Tablas de Paula et al.\ propose an event-prediction encoder for sound
matching with a differentiable Karplus--Strong algorithm
\cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. Their experiments show, however, that standard
audio losses provide unreliable joint gradients for event onsets and
fundamental frequencies. Parameter supervision on synthetic sounds performs
well in-domain but does not transfer reliably to recorded instruments, for
which externally estimated pitch and onset remain more effective.

This failure is not primarily an encoder problem. Fundamental frequency and
onset are displacement parameters: changing either one relocates coherent
signal energy instead of merely changing its amplitude. A frequency
perturbation changes phase throughout the duration of a note, producing an
oscillatory waveform-distance surface, while pointwise spectral distances
compare energy only between corresponding time--frequency bins. A candidate
can therefore receive a weak or misleading gradient whenever its energy does
not overlap that of the target \cite{turian2020loss,hayes2023sinusoidal}. This
helps explain why $f_0$ is commonly supplied by an external pitch estimator in
DDSP systems, or learned using parameter labels from synthesizer-generated
data \cite{engel2020inverse}. These strategies bypass the problematic
audio-loss geometry: an externally supplied estimate is no longer adjustable
by the reconstruction objective, whereas synthetic parameter supervision can
introduce a simulation-to-recording generalisation gap.

Optimal transport has been proposed to address this problem by measuring the
displacement of spectral energy rather than comparing fixed coordinates.
Spectral optimal transport (\sot) moves energy along frequency within each
analysis frame \cite{torres2024sot}, while joint time--frequency optimal
transport (\tfw) also permits transport between times
\cite{fabiani2024jtfot}. The latter is particularly relevant to event-based
sound matching because both pitch and onset must be recovered. Nevertheless,
its behaviour depends on the metric and relative scaling assigned to the time
and frequency coordinates.

In this work, we introduce the bidirectional cumulative-energy loss (\bicul),
which compares normalised cumulative spectral-power surfaces in both
directions of both time and frequency. This cumulative representation provides
a global indication of the direction in which displaced energy must move,
without using an $f_0$ estimator, onset detector, or information about the
target event parameters. We evaluate whether this produces better gradient
fields and more accurate recovery of pluck onsets and pitches than waveform,
spectral, and optimal-transport objectives as the number of events in a phrase
increases. By optimising the event parameters directly, rather than training an
encoder, the experiments isolate the geometry supplied by the renderer and
loss.

We use a differentiable digital waveguide because it brings these questions
together in one compact model. A waveguide produces a timbrically rich,
decaying plucked-string sound from an excitation, short delay lines, filters,
and recursive state \cite{valimaki1996plucked,depaula2026waveguides}. The
excitation time determines the onset of a pluck, while the propagation delay
determines its fundamental frequency. Successive events can also interact
through the state of the string. The model therefore exhibits meaningful
time-varying behaviour using only a small number of event parameters, rather
than requiring densely sampled control trajectories.

A suitable loss is necessary but not sufficient for recovering those
parameters. The final parameter gradient also depends on the Jacobian of the
renderer. Sub-sample onset placement determines how a continuous event time
enters the discrete-time excitation, while fractional-delay interpolation
determines how a continuous delay-line length is mapped to the waveguide
samples. Numerical methods with similar forward responses may consequently
produce substantially different derivatives. Tablas de Paula et al.\ already
observe differences between frequency-sampled and time-domain fractional
delays \cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. We investigate this effect
systematically by crossing four onset-placement methods with four
fractional-delay realisations and measuring whether their pitch and onset
gradients point towards the target.

Accordingly, this work makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate differentiable phrase matching using sparse, musically
    meaningful pluck events, providing a controlled connection between audio
    synthesis parameters and symbolic note-event representations.
    \item We introduce \bicul{} and compare its pitch and onset gradient
    geometry and phrase-recovery performance against waveform, spectral, and
    optimal-transport losses over phrases containing increasingly many plucks.
    \item We systematically evaluate how sub-sample onset placement,
    waveguide fractional-delay interpolation, and recursive state affect the
    direction of gradients with respect to event timing and fundamental
    frequency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Cumulative energy losses}
\label{sec:losses}
Let $x$ be a target, let $\theta$ collect the candidate event coordinates, and
let $\hat x=R_\rho(\theta)$ be the audio produced by renderer realisation
$\rho$. A loss can be defined on $(x,\hat x)$ without reference to the
synthesizer, but its event-parameter gradient also contains the renderer
Jacobian:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(x,R_\rho(\theta))=
J_{R_\rho}(\theta)^\mathsf{T}\nabla_{\hat x}\mathcal L(x,\hat x).
\label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
We therefore study two controlled slices: Fig.~\ref{fig:landscapes} fixes
$\rho$ and varies the loss, whereas Table~\ref{tab:interpolation-onset} fixes
\bicul{} and varies $\rho$.

\subsection{Reference objectives}
\lone{} is mean absolute sample error and \ltwo{} is mean squared sample
error. For \mss{}, we follow Schw\"ar and M\"uller's smooth configuration
\cite{schwar2023mss}. Their periodic flat-top windows suppress the sidelobes
responsible for spectral-leakage fluctuations, while using the prime lengths
$\{17,31,67,127,257,509\}$ prevents several resolutions from aligning their
DFT bins. The corresponding hops are $\{8,15,33,63,128,254\}$; features are
$\log(1+|\operatorname{STFT}|)$ magnitudes, compared by a summed squared
$\mathcal{L}_2$ distance.

For \sot{}, we use the implementation of Torres et al.\
\cite{torres2024sot}. It combines a frame-normalised transport term on
FFT-512/hop-64 flat-top-windowed STFT power, including their unbalanced
quantile cutoff, with 0.05 times a Hann-windowed, linear-magnitude MSS term.
For \tfw{}, we follow Fabiani et al.\ \cite{fabiani2024jtfot}: a
periodic-Hann FFT-256/hop-128 magnitude STFT is globally unit-normalised and
projected along ten fixed angles. Frequency is measured in Hz and the time
coordinate is $\widetilde t=1000t$ in Hz, so 1~s equals 1000~Hz.

\subsection{Bidirectional cumulative energy}
Let $P$ and $\hat P$ be periodic-Hann FFT-256/hop-64 STFT power. For time
direction $d_t$ and frequency direction $d_f$,
\begin{equation}
S_{d_t,d_f}=C^f_{d_f}\!\left(C^t_{d_t}(P)\right),\qquad
\hat S_{d_t,d_f}=C^f_{d_f}\!\left(C^t_{d_t}(\hat P)\right).
\end{equation}
Each directional pair uses the target maximum $m=\max S_{d_t,d_f}$:
\begin{align}
R&=\sqrt{\max(S_{d_t,d_f}/m,10^{-12})},\\
\hat R&=\sqrt{\max(\hat S_{d_t,d_f}/m,10^{-12})},\\
\ell_{d_t,d_f}&=\operatorname{RMS}(R-\hat R).
\end{align}
\bicul{} averages the four choices
$d_t\in\{\rightarrow,\leftarrow\}$ and
$d_f\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$. A displaced energy component changes every
cumulative value between its location and the corresponding boundary. The
forward and backward time sums therefore expose whether candidate energy is
too early or too late, while the upward and downward frequency sums expose
whether it is too low or too high. Averaging all four orientations avoids
privileging a particular boundary. No event coordinates, detectors, or
salience weights enter the construction.

\subsection{Conditional loss geometry}
Although these objectives are renderer-independent, their geometry with
respect to $t$ and $f_0$ is not. To compare them under controlled conditions,
Fig.~\ref{fig:landscapes} holds the renderer fixed to the Fourier-onset/Thiran-
propagation realisation defined and qualified in Sec.~\ref{sec:renderer}. It
uses a 160-Hz event at 1.0 s on an exact 25-by-25 lattice: time is linear over
a $\pm0.8$-s shift and pitch logarithmic over a $\pm1$-octave shift, with the
exact target inserted at the centre. Each surface is independently min--max
normalised. White arrows show the autograd unit-descent direction

\section{Differentiable phrase renderer}
\label{sec:renderer}
We now instantiate $R_\rho$. The physical topology is fixed independently of
the numerical realisation studied below. Figure~\ref{fig:waveguide} implements
a digital waveguide as two oppositely travelling rails. Introducing the pluck
at a point along the string divides each rail into a pluck--nut and a
pluck--bridge section, giving four one-way delay sections; the sum of the two
section lengths determines $f_0$, while their ratio determines pluck position
\cite{depaula2026waveguides}. We set this ratio to 0.23 measured from the
bridge. A rigid nut reflects with gain $-1$, and the bridge reflection is
$-H_L(z)$ with
\begin{equation}
H_L(z)=g\frac{1-a_1}{1-a_1z^{-1}},\qquad g=0.99,\quad a_1=0.2.
\end{equation}
Here $g$ is the low-frequency round-trip gain, while $a_1$ controls the
increasing attenuation of higher frequencies. The chosen values produce a
timbrally complex, decaying string response whose RT60 also varies with
$f_0$, because higher pitches complete more round trips per second; this
dependence is illustrated in Fig.~3 of Tablas de Paula et al.\
\cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. The output is the bridge-incident travelling
velocity.

\subsection{Continuous event rendering}
Audio is float64, two seconds long, and sampled at 4 kHz. A zero-onset
half-raised-cosine (HRC) prototype is
\begin{equation}
e_0(t)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{A_e}{2}[1-\cos(\pi t/T_e)],&0\leq t<T_e,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where the excitation amplitude and duration are fixed to $A_e=0.8$ and
$T_e=10$ ms. The onset comparison applies four continuous shifts to this
prototype. Naive placement samples the analytic HRC at the requested onset;
Lagrange uses a six-tap, fifth-order maximally-flat FIR shift; Fourier uses the
delay response $e^{-j\omega D}$ implemented in FLAMO
\cite{dalsanto2025flamo}; and Thiran uses a causal first-order all-pass shift.

We write a propagation delay as $\mathcal D_p(D;z)\approx z^{-D}$. Linear uses
two-tap, order-1 Lagrange sections on both rails; Lagrange uses order 5 on the
long pluck--nut section and order 1 on the short pluck--bridge section; Fourier
samples the delay responses without a polynomial interpolator; and Thiran uses
order 3 on the long section and order 1 on the short section. Thiran preserves
magnitude while approximating the requested group delay. As described by
Laakso et al.\ \cite{laakso1996splitting}, Lagrange and Thiran are
corresponding maximally-flat FIR and all-pass designs, but their higher-order
forms require a sufficiently long realizable delay. The chosen pluck position
can make the bridge-side section shorter than those requirements, motivating
the order-1 short section for both methods. Every rail is tuned using the
realised fractional-delay and one-pole-filter phase.

\subsection{State policy and recursive realisation}
Each constant event regime is reduced algebraically to an exact rational
transfer. We evaluate the recurrent IIR filter and backpropagate through it
with PhilTorch's direct-form-II (DF2) interface
\cite{yu2025philtorch}, using its TorchLPC backend. The collapsed DF2 transfer
compresses the rail delays, reflections, and bridge termination into one
history, which is reinterpreted when coefficients change. The Literal
realisation instead retains separate histories on the physical right- and
left-going rail sections and bridge filter. They are equivalent for fixed
coefficients but need not remain so under a parameter switch.

The hard-reset policy clears every relevant history at an event
boundary before processing the new excitation. The persistent diagnostic
instead carries history across the switch. Figure~\ref{fig:state-realization}
uses the target phrase $(110,0.5),(160,1.0),(280,1.5)$ in Hz and seconds and
sweeps only the middle event. Reset produces the same smooth \bicul{} sweep in
both recursive realisations, whereas both persistent sweeps are rugged.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Targets and optimisation}
Master seed 2027 generates 150 scrambled-Latin-hypercube targets for each of
one, two, four, six, and eight events. Pitches are log-mapped to 80--320 Hz.
All target and candidate onsets lie between 0.2 and 1.8 s; target events are
separated by at least 50 ms. Candidate pitches are initialised at 160 Hz, and
their onsets at the centres of equal-width cells spanning the allowed time
interval. Pitch and onset use independent elementwise bounded logits, and all
event coordinates are optimised independently.

We use Adam with learning rate 0.05. During optimisation, each loss is divided
by its value at initialisation so that objectives of different numerical scale
receive comparable step sizes; this factor does not change the reported loss.
A relative raw-loss decrease of $10^{-4}$ resets patience. After 100
evaluations without such improvement, the strict best is restored, the
learning rate is multiplied by 0.3 down to $10^{-5}$, and the optimiser state
is cleared. Optimisation stops after 250 further evaluations or 3000 updates
and retains the lowest raw-loss iterate.

\subsection{Coordinate variants and evaluation}
The time--frequency transport row labelled $1\,\mathrm{s}=1\,\mathrm{oct}$
changes only the coordinates of Fabiani et al.'s \tfw{}: frequency becomes
$\log_2[\max(f,20)/20]$ octaves and time is measured in seconds, giving equal
coordinate distance to one second and one octave. This is the transport
variant used in the six-loss study.

The exploratory Log-Q variant changes only the final RMS in \bicul. Whereas
canonical \bicul{} weights every point of the rectangular STFT-bin lattice
equally, Log-Q approximates integration in physical coordinates: seconds
along time and $\log_2[\max(f,20)/20]$ octaves along frequency. For
$E_{d_t,d_f}=\hat R-R$, it uses
\begin{equation}
\ell^{\rm quad}_{d_t,d_f}=\left[\frac{\sum_{k,n}
w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}E_{d_t,d_f}(k,n)^2}
{\sum_{k,n}w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}}\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where each $w$ is the adjacent-coordinate interval aligned with the
cumulative direction. The cumulative surfaces, target scaling, and square-root
transform remain unchanged.

The six-loss study contains 4500 fits. The original linear-Hz \tfw{} of
Fabiani et al. and Log-Q were evaluated separately on the same targets, adding
750 fits each and giving 6000 fits across 40 conditions. These two extensions
are excluded from the confirmatory test family. No objective uses spectral
pre-emphasis. Hungarian assignment minimises
\begin{equation}
d_{ij}=\left[\frac{\log_2(\hat f_i/f_j)}{2}\right]^2+
\left[\frac{\hat t_i-t_j}{1.6}\right]^2.
\end{equation}
We compute within-phrase mean absolute cents and onset milliseconds after
assignment, then report their median across phrases. Periodic-Hann
FFT-256/hop-64 log-spectral distance (LSD) supplies an objective-independent
audio summary, shown as its complete per-phrase distribution.

in every comparison. After correction, all five LSD comparisons and both
pitch and onset comparisons for four, six, and eight events rejected their
nulls (11/15); pitch and onset for one and two events did not.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
Targets are synthetic; the number of events is known; and target and candidate share
excitation, damping, and waveguide structure. The 4-kHz rate restricts
bandwidth, Thiran sections approximate ideal propagation delay, the finite
Fourier onset shift has small periodic pre-ringing, and hard reset is an
abstraction. The confirmatory inference is restricted to the preregistered
pairing of \bicul{} and the octave-coordinate transport variant; the
eight-loss ranks are descriptive.
Unknown cardinality, real recordings, dynamics, pickup position, decay,
vibrato, slides, polyphony, and instrument-specific claims remain outside the
present evidence.

We formulate phrase analysis as direct recovery of event pitch and onset and
introduce a loss that compares cumulative STFT power in both directions of
both axes. The signed 6000-fit study provides physical-unit errors and audio
distributions under one controlled renderer. Its powered paired analysis
supports canonical \bicul{} over the octave-coordinate transport variant
in 11 of 15 corrected tests, while the log-frequency-quadrature variant of
\bicul{} improves the descriptive median pitch and onset recovery at four,
six, and eight events and remains a hypothesis for independent study.
