\begin{abstract}
Differentiable digital signal processing enables interpretable, label-free
analysis by synthesis, but fundamental frequency and event onset are unusually
difficult to recover directly from audio. Changing either translates coherent
energy, whereas waveform and pointwise spectral losses do not encode the
direction or extent of that displacement. Systems therefore often fix these
quantities using external detectors, preventing the reconstruction objective
from correcting them. We instead represent a monophonic plucked-string phrase
as a sparse sequence of plucks, each parameterised by one onset and one
fundamental frequency, and optimise them through a differentiable digital
waveguide. Its compact feedback-delay structure yields rich, stateful,
pitch-controlled signals while exposing the effects of onset placement and
fractional-delay realisation. We introduce a bidirectional cumulative-energy
loss that compares normalised spectral-power accumulations along both
directions of time and frequency, thereby retaining directional information
about displaced energy. In a 6000-fit analysis-by-synthesis study of phrases
containing one to eight plucks, it recovers onset and pitch more reliably than
waveform, spectral, framewise optimal-transport, and joint time--frequency
optimal-transport objectives. It is also the only evaluated objective whose
descent direction points towards the target at every sampled point in the
single-pluck landscape. Finally, crossing four onset-placement and four
delay-interpolation methods shows that gradients depend strongly on numerical
realisation; Fourier pluck placement with causal Thiran propagation is the
most consistently target-directed combination. These results support direct
audio-supervised optimisation of pitch, event timing, and delay-line length.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Differentiable digital signal processing (\ddsp) places interpretable audio
processors inside an automatic-differentiation graph, enabling
analysis by synthesis, in which synthesizer controls minimise an audio
reconstruction objective \cite{engel2020ddsp,caspe2022ddx7}. Sound-matching
representations nevertheless tend towards two temporal extremes: a global
parameter vector for a fixed or approximately linear time-invariant processor,
or dense frame-rate trajectories, as in DDSP resynthesis and differentiable
frequency-modulation synthesis. Dense control suits continuously driven
instruments and evolving modulation, but is unnecessarily high-dimensional
when the principal musical changes occur at a few discrete instants.

Music Information Retrieval (MIR), by contrast, describes performances
symbolically through note events with attributes such as onset, pitch,
duration, and velocity \cite{hawthorne2018onsets}. For plucked and struck
instruments, this suggests the intermediate audio-domain representation
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}=\{(t_i,f_{0,i})\}_{i=1}^{C},
\end{equation}
where pluck $i$ has onset $t_i$ and fundamental frequency $f_{0,i}$; other
controls need not remain fixed between plucks. This connects audio-domain
sound matching to note-centred representations without imposing a dense
trajectory. Related approaches seek compact structure between global and
framewise controls. Mitcheltree et al.\ represent synthesizer modulation with
constrained curves and piecewise B\'ezier splines
\cite{mitcheltree2025modulation}. More directly, Tablas de Paula et al.\ use an
event-prediction encoder with a differentiable Karplus--Strong synthesizer
\cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. They find, however, that standard audio losses
give unreliable joint onset and pitch gradients, while synthetic parameter
supervision does not transfer reliably to recordings. An event-based
parameterisation alone therefore does not solve the estimation problem.

The underlying difficulty is geometric. Fundamental frequency and onset are
displacement parameters: changing either relocates coherent energy rather than
only its amplitude. Frequency perturbations alter phase throughout a note,
making waveform-distance surfaces oscillatory, while pointwise spectral
distances compare only corresponding time--frequency bins. Gradients can thus
be weak or misleading when candidate and target energy do not overlap
\cite{turian2020loss,hayes2023sinusoidal}. Many DDSP systems bypass this
geometry by supplying $f_0$ from an external estimator or learning it from
synthetic parameter labels \cite{engel2020inverse}; the former prevents the
audio objective from correcting the estimate, and the latter risks a
simulation-to-recording gap.

Optimal transport has been proposed to address this problem by measuring the
displacement of spectral energy rather than comparing fixed coordinates.
Spectral optimal transport (\sot) moves energy along frequency within each
analysis frame \cite{torres2024sot}, while joint time--frequency optimal
transport (\tfw) also permits transport between times
\cite{fabiani2024jtfot}. The latter is relevant when both pitch and onset must
be recovered, but depends on the metric and relative scale assigned to its two
coordinates. We instead introduce the bidirectional cumulative-energy loss
(\bicul), which compares normalised spectral-power accumulations in both
directions of both axes. It indicates the direction of displaced energy
without an $f_0$ estimator, onset detector, or target-event coordinates.

We evaluate these losses by directly optimising events through a
differentiable digital waveguide. Its excitation time sets pluck onset, its
propagation delay sets $f_0$, and its recursive state couples successive
events. It consequently produces a rich, decaying plucked-string signal from
few parameters \cite{valimaki1996plucked,depaula2026waveguides}, allowing loss
and renderer geometry to be isolated without a learned encoder.

A suitable loss is necessary but not sufficient for recovering those
parameters. The final parameter gradient also depends on the Jacobian of the
renderer. Sub-sample onset placement determines how a continuous event time
enters the discrete-time excitation, while fractional-delay interpolation
determines how a continuous delay-line length is mapped to the waveguide
samples. Numerical methods with similar forward responses may consequently
produce different derivatives. Following initial evidence for this effect in
frequency-sampled and time-domain fractional delays
\cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}, we cross four onset-placement methods with
four fractional-delay realisations and measure whether their pitch and onset
gradients point towards the target.

Accordingly, this work makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an event-based formulation for direct recovery of pluck onset and
    pitch from audio;
    \item \bicul{} and its gradient geometry and phrase-recovery comparison
    with waveform, spectral, and optimal-transport objectives; and
    \item a controlled evaluation of how onset placement, fractional-delay
    interpolation, and recursive state affect event-parameter gradients.
\end{itemize}

\section{Cumulative energy losses}
\label{sec:losses}
Let $x$ be a target, let $\theta$ collect the candidate event coordinates, and
let $\hat x=R_\rho(\theta)$ be the audio produced by renderer realisation
$\rho$. A loss can be defined on $(x,\hat x)$ without reference to the
synthesizer, but its event-parameter gradient also contains the renderer
Jacobian:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(x,R_\rho(\theta))=
J_{R_\rho}(\theta)^\mathsf{T}\nabla_{\hat x}\mathcal L(x,\hat x).
\label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
We therefore study two controlled slices: Fig.~\ref{fig:landscapes} fixes
$\rho$ and varies the loss, whereas Table~\ref{tab:interpolation-onset} fixes
\bicul{} and varies $\rho$.

\subsection{Reference objectives}
\lone{} is mean absolute sample error and \ltwo{} is mean squared sample
error. For \mss{}, we follow Schw\"ar and M\"uller's smooth configuration
\cite{schwar2023mss}. Their periodic flat-top windows suppress the sidelobes
responsible for spectral-leakage fluctuations, while using the prime lengths
$\{17,31,67,127,257,509\}$ prevents several resolutions from aligning their
DFT bins. The corresponding hops are $\{8,15,33,63,128,254\}$; features are
$\log(1+|\operatorname{STFT}|)$ magnitudes, compared by a summed squared
$\mathcal{L}_2$ distance.

For \sot{}, we use the implementation of Torres et al.\
\cite{torres2024sot}. It combines a frame-normalised transport term on
FFT-512/hop-64 flat-top-windowed STFT power, including their unbalanced
quantile cutoff, with 0.05 times a Hann-windowed, linear-magnitude MSS term.
For \tfw{}, we follow Fabiani et al.\ \cite{fabiani2024jtfot}: a
periodic-Hann FFT-256/hop-128 magnitude STFT is globally unit-normalised and
projected along ten fixed angles. Frequency is measured in Hz and the time
coordinate is $\widetilde t=1000t$ in Hz, so 1~s equals 1000~Hz.

\subsection{Bidirectional cumulative energy}
Let $P$ and $\hat P$ be periodic-Hann FFT-256/hop-64 STFT power. For time
direction $d_t$ and frequency direction $d_f$,
\begin{equation}
S_{d_t,d_f}=C^f_{d_f}\!\left(C^t_{d_t}(P)\right),\qquad
\hat S_{d_t,d_f}=C^f_{d_f}\!\left(C^t_{d_t}(\hat P)\right).
\end{equation}
Each directional pair uses the target maximum $m=\max S_{d_t,d_f}$:
\begin{align}
R&=\sqrt{\max(S_{d_t,d_f}/m,10^{-12})},\\
\hat R&=\sqrt{\max(\hat S_{d_t,d_f}/m,10^{-12})},\\
\ell_{d_t,d_f}&=\operatorname{RMS}(R-\hat R).
\end{align}
\bicul{} averages the four choices
$d_t\in\{\rightarrow,\leftarrow\}$ and
$d_f\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$. A displaced energy component changes every
cumulative value between its location and the corresponding boundary. The
forward and backward time sums therefore expose whether candidate energy is
too early or too late, while the upward and downward frequency sums expose
whether it is too low or too high. Averaging all four orientations avoids
privileging a particular boundary. No event coordinates, detectors, or
salience weights enter the construction.

\section{Differentiable phrase renderer}
\label{sec:renderer}
We now instantiate $R_\rho$. The physical topology is fixed independently of
the numerical realisation studied below. Figure~\ref{fig:waveguide} implements
a digital waveguide as two oppositely travelling rails. Introducing the pluck
at a point along the string divides each rail into a pluck--nut and a
pluck--bridge section, giving four one-way delay sections; the sum of the two
section lengths determines $f_0$, while their ratio determines pluck position
\cite{depaula2026waveguides}. We set this ratio to 0.23 measured from the
bridge. A rigid nut reflects with gain $-1$, and the bridge reflection is
$-H_L(z)$ with
\begin{equation}
H_L(z)=g\frac{1-a_1}{1-a_1z^{-1}},\qquad g=0.99,\quad a_1=0.2.
\end{equation}
Here $g$ is the low-frequency round-trip gain, while $a_1$ controls the
increasing attenuation of higher frequencies. The chosen values produce a
timbrally complex, decaying string response whose RT60 also varies with
$f_0$, because higher pitches complete more round trips per second; this
dependence is illustrated in Fig.~3 of Tablas de Paula et al.\
\cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. The output is the bridge-incident travelling
velocity.

\subsection{Differentiable onset placement and interpolation}
Audio is float64, two seconds long, and sampled at 4 kHz. A zero-onset
half-raised-cosine (HRC) prototype is
\begin{equation}
e_0(t)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{A_e}{2}[1-\cos(\pi t/T_e)],&0\leq t<T_e,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where the excitation amplitude and duration are fixed to $A_e=0.8$ and
$T_e=10$ ms. The onset comparison applies four continuous shifts to this
prototype. Naive placement samples the analytic HRC at the requested onset;
Lagrange uses a six-tap, fifth-order maximally-flat FIR shift; Fourier uses the
delay response $e^{-j\omega D}$ implemented in FLAMO
\cite{dalsanto2025flamo}; and Thiran uses a causal first-order all-pass shift.

We write a propagation delay as $\mathcal D_p(D;z)\approx z^{-D}$. Linear uses
two-tap, order-1 Lagrange sections on both rails; Lagrange uses order 5 on the
long pluck--nut section and order 1 on the short pluck--bridge section; Fourier
samples the delay responses without a polynomial interpolator; and Thiran uses
order 3 on the long section and order 1 on the short section. Thiran preserves
magnitude while approximating the requested group delay. As described by
Laakso et al.\ \cite{laakso1996splitting}, Lagrange and Thiran are
corresponding maximally-flat FIR and all-pass designs, but their higher-order
forms require a sufficiently long realizable delay. The chosen pluck position
can make the bridge-side section shorter than those requirements, motivating
the order-1 short section for both methods. Every rail is tuned using the
realised fractional-delay and one-pole-filter phase.

Table~\ref{tab:interpolation-onset} is the complementary slice to
Fig.~\ref{fig:landscapes}: it fixes \bicul{} and varies onset placement and
propagation interpolation on the same 25-by-25 lattice. Target and candidate
share the named construction in each cell, so the comparison measures its
derivative geometry rather than a renderer mismatch. For the Fourier
propagation column, the waveguide is assembled as a FLAMO module
\cite{dalsanto2025flamo}. Its 65,536-point transform is 8.192 times the
retained 8000-sample horizon to suppress circular time aliasing, and real DC
and Nyquist bins with conjugate negative-frequency completion enforce
Hermitian symmetry.

Table~\ref{tab:interpolation-onset} identifies Fourier onset placement with

resampling, while Thiran supplies causal, stateful, all-pass propagation. We

\subsection{Phase interference and string reset}
Each constant event regime can be collapsed algebraically to an exact rational
transfer, which we evaluate and differentiate using PhilTorch's direct-form-II
(DF2) interface and TorchLPC backend \cite{yu2025philtorch}. As Yu et al.'s
TorchLPC study notes, filtering order matters in time-varying systems:
rearrangements that are equivalent for fixed coefficients need not remain so
once the coefficients change \cite{yu2024torchlpc}. Our collapsed DF2
realisation compresses the rail delays, reflections, and bridge termination
into one history, whose values are reinterpreted by the new coefficients at an
event boundary. To test this collapse, the Literal realisation evaluates the
uncollapsed digital waveguide and retains separate histories for its physical
right- and left-going rail sections and bridge filter. The two agree within a
constant regime but need not agree across an event boundary.

Figure~\ref{fig:state-realization} sweeps the middle event in a three-pluck
target: 110 Hz at 0.5 s, 160 Hz at 1.0 s, and 280 Hz at 1.5 s. With persistent
state, DF2 and Literal produce different slopes, but both landscapes remain
rugged and their gradients repeatedly turn away from the target. Resetting the
string---clearing every filter and delay-line history at each event boundary
before applying the new excitation---makes the realisations agree and restores
smooth, target-directed slopes. We therefore use string reset for all remaining
experiments. We hypothesise that the carried oscillation and new pluck alternate
between constructive and destructive phase interference as the swept coordinate
changes, corrugating the loss; future work should isolate this mechanism.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Targets}
Master seed 2027 generates 150 scrambled-Latin-hypercube targets for each of
one, two, four, six, and eight events. Pitches are log-mapped to 80--320 Hz.
Target onsets lie between 0.2 and 1.8 s and are separated by at least 50 ms.

\subsection{Optimisation}
Candidate pitches are initialised at 160 Hz, and their onsets at the centres of
equal-width cells spanning 0.2--1.8 s. Pitch and onset use independent
elementwise bounded logits, and all event coordinates are optimised
independently.
We use Adam with learning rate 0.05. During optimisation, each loss is divided
by its value at initialisation so that objectives of different numerical scale
receive comparable step sizes; this factor does not change the reported loss.
A relative raw-loss decrease of $10^{-4}$ resets patience. After 100
evaluations without such improvement, the strict best is restored, the
learning rate is multiplied by 0.3 down to $10^{-5}$, and the optimiser state
is cleared. Optimisation stops after 250 further evaluations or 3000 updates
and retains the lowest raw-loss iterate.

\subsection{Loss variants}
The time--frequency transport row labelled $1\,\mathrm{s}=1\,\mathrm{oct}$
changes only the coordinates of Fabiani et al.'s \tfw{}: frequency becomes
$\log_2[\max(f,20)/20]$ octaves and time is measured in seconds, giving equal
coordinate distance to one second and one octave.

The Log-Q variant changes only the final RMS in \bicul. Whereas \bicul{}
weights every point of the rectangular STFT-bin lattice equally, Log-Q
approximates integration in physical coordinates: seconds along time and
$\log_2[\max(f,20)/20]$ octaves along frequency. For
$E_{d_t,d_f}=\hat R-R$, it uses
\begin{equation}
\ell^{\rm quad}_{d_t,d_f}=\left[\frac{\sum_{k,n}
w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}E_{d_t,d_f}(k,n)^2}
{\sum_{k,n}w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}}\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where each $w$ is the adjacent-coordinate interval aligned with the
cumulative direction. The cumulative surfaces, target scaling, and square-root
transform remain unchanged.

Together, the six objectives in Sec.~\ref{sec:losses} and these two variants
form an eight-loss study: 150 targets at five event counts give 6000 fits
across 40 loss--event-count conditions.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Predicted events are unordered, so Hungarian assignment pairs them one-to-one
with target events by minimising
\begin{equation}
d_{ij}=\left[\frac{\log_2(\hat f_i/f_j)}{2}\right]^2+
\left[\frac{\hat t_i-t_j}{1.6}\right]^2.
\end{equation}
We compute within-phrase mean absolute cents and onset milliseconds after
assignment, then report their median across phrases. Periodic-Hann
FFT-256/hop-64 log-spectral distance (LSD) supplies an objective-independent
audio summary, shown as its complete per-phrase distribution.

We compare \bicul{} with the octave-coordinate transport variant for cents,
onset, and LSD at five event counts, giving 15 paired two-sided tests. A
noncentral-$t$ power calculation with Bonferroni familywise $\alpha=0.05$,
paired $d_z=0.35$, and 90\% power requires 150 phrases per event count (power
0.901 at $n=150$ and 0.899 at $n=149$). All tests use the exact shared phrases.
\bicul{} has the lower mean in every comparison. After correction, all five
LSD comparisons and both pitch and onset comparisons for four, six, and eight
events reject their nulls (11/15); pitch and onset for one and two events do
not.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
Targets are synthetic; the number of events is known; and target and candidate share
excitation, damping, and waveguide structure. The 4-kHz rate restricts
bandwidth, Thiran sections approximate ideal propagation delay, the finite
Fourier onset shift has small periodic pre-ringing, and hard reset is an
abstraction.
Unknown cardinality, real recordings, dynamics, pickup position, decay,
vibrato, slides, polyphony, and instrument-specific claims remain outside the
present evidence.

We formulate phrase analysis as direct recovery of event pitch and onset and
introduce a loss that compares cumulative STFT power in both directions of
both axes. The signed 6000-fit study provides physical-unit errors and audio
distributions under one controlled renderer. Its powered paired analysis
supports \bicul{} over the octave-coordinate transport variant in 11 of 15
corrected tests, while Log-Q improves median pitch and onset recovery at four,
six, and eight events.
