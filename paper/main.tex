\begin{abstract}
\acrlong{ddsp} enables interpretable, unsupervised recovery of sound processor parameters via analysis by synthesis. However, it relies on waveform and spectral pointwise losses that do not encode energy displacement, needed to obtain essential parameters like onset time and pitch. On this work, we propose the use of \glspl{cel}, which compare \acrshort{stft} power accumulated in time and frequency directions. We use them to recover the onset and pitch of sparse plucks rendered by a differentiable digital waveguide whose delay length defines the pluck's pitch, in the process recovering musical phrases. In a single-pluck landscape, we find our proposed losses have gradients that point towards the target at 120 equally spaced coordinates. Across 6000 fits containing one to eight pluck phrase fits, our proposed losses improve joint onset--pitch recovery over traditional waveform, spectral pointwise and recently proposed \acrlong{ot} objectives. We believe these losses will further support unsupervised optimisation of onset timing, pitch and delay line length, and provide a PyTorch library for ease of use in future applications\footnote{\textcolor{red}{link to library should be here}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\glsresetall
Engel et al. coined the term \Gls{ddsp}, as the process of embedding audio processors in automatic differentiation graphs for efficient and interpretable unsupervised analysis by synthesis~\cite{engel2020ddsp}. Since then, it has been widely adopted in multitude of applications, such as neural waveshaping, \gls{fm} synthesis, reverberation, vocal tract modelling and effects modulation ~\cite{hayes2021waveshaping, caspe2022ddx7, dalsanto2023diffdn, sudholt2023vocal, yu2024torchlpc, mitcheltree2025modulation, carson2026gradient}; for an early comprehensive review, we redirect the reader to Hayes et al.~\cite{hayes2024review}.

In \gls{ddsp}, controls are mostly predicted as either a constant set of parameters, or dense frame trajectories, naturally fit for \gls{ir} prediction or continuously driven sounds respectively. However, that is not how many instruments are instinctively performed. Previously, we proposed an event-set predictor for the recovery of sparse guitar plucks \cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}, forming note events with onset, pitch ($f_0$) and velocity among other physical parameters. This work expands this framework with the purpose of joining common \gls{mir} representations, where plucked string is represented as \gls{midi}, sheet music or guitar tablatures ~\cite{riley2024gaps, loth2025goat,loth2025guitarflow} with \gls{ddsp}.

However, $f_0$ and onset time, representing a displacement of energy, are notoriously difficult to predict. This is due to the fact that~\gls{ddsp}     relies on waveform and pointwise spectral losses, such as \gls{mss}, prompt to oscillatory local minima and useless when there is no energy overlap \cite{wang2019nsf, turian2020loss}. Recent revisions of \gls{mss} aimed to reduce the former issue by reducing spectral leaking \cite{schwar2023mss}, but do not address the latter. Complex surrogates were proposed but remain synthesiser-dependant \cite{hayes2023sinusoidal}. The literature commonly eludes this problematic via external detectors or synthetic labels, the first inherit the detectors' errors and veto further end-to-end training of $f_0$ \cite{engel2020inverse, engel2020ddsp, caspe2022ddx7, sudholt2023vocal}, while the latter generalises poorly to out of domain data \cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}.

Recent years have seen the adoption of \gls{ot}, which measures the shortest energy displacement cost, in machine learning \cite{monge1781deblais,cuturi2013sinkhorn,peyre2019computational} and audio applications, such as music transcription and source separation \cite{flamary2016optimal, rolet2018blind}. Torres et al. were the first to apply it to \gls{ddsp} for harmonic parameter estimation, as a frame-wise spectral $\mathcal{W}_2$, \gls{sot} \cite{torres2024sot}; followingly, Fabiani et al. proposed a sliced formulation of a joint time--frequency \gls{ot} as a similarity metric, the \gls{tfw} ~\cite{fabiani2024jtfot}.

While promising, we showed that \gls{sot} is unreliable for joint onset--$f_0$ optimisation, and proposed \gls{tfw} as future work \cite{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. Hence, we propose recovering musical phrases via optimisation of $f_0$ and onset time of plucks rendered by a simple digital waveguide \cite{smith2010physical}, comparing \gls{tfw} with a newly proposed family of losses, \glspl{cel}, which compare \gls{stft} power accumulation in time and frequency bins.

\section{Cumulative Energy Losses}
\label{sec:losses}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\PaperRoot/figures/cumulative_surfaces.pdf}
\caption{STFT power of a constant amplitude logarithmic 20-to-1000~Hz sine sweep over two seconds, and its resulting cumulative surfaces for the direction pairs $(d_t,d_f)$.
The shared linear colour scale runs from 0 to 1, with power normalised by peak
power for the spectrogram and total target power ($S_q/m$) for the
cumulative-power surfaces. Logarithmic frequency axes are used for display
only; accumulation uses linear power on the original STFT grid. Arrows mark
starting corners and taccumulation directions.}
\label{fig:cumulative-surfaces}
\end{figure*}
We propose a new family of losses, \Glspl{cel}, that compare accumulated
spectral power, exposing displacement through changes between the original
and shifted energy. Let $P_{k,n}$ and $\hat P_{k,n}$ denote target and candidate
\gls{stft} power at frequency bin $k$ and frame $n$, using a periodic Hann
window, a 256-point \gls{fft}, and a 64-sample hop.

For a chosen accumulation direction in time, $d_t$, we read frames from
first to last ($\rightarrow$) or last to first ($\leftarrow$). Similarly,
the frequency direction $d_f$ reads bins from low to high ($\uparrow$)
or high to low ($\downarrow$). A direction pair $q=(d_t,d_f)$ combines
one choice for each axis, giving four possible pairs.
For each pair, the cumulative-sum operator $C^t_{d_t}$ replaces each bin's
power by the sum of that power and all preceding frames at the same frequency
in the chosen time direction. The operator $C^f_{d_f}$ performs the corresponding cumulative sum
along frequency. Applying both operators gives the target and candidate
cumulative-power surfaces, $S_q$ and $\hat S_q$:
\begin{equation*}
S_q=C^f_{d_f}(C^t_{d_t}(P)),\qquad
\hat S_q=C^f_{d_f}(C^t_{d_t}(\hat P)).
\end{equation*}
Each surface is a two-dimensional image used to compute the loss. Its value
at $(k,n)$ contains the power in the rectangle from the chosen starting
corner to that bin inclusive, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cumulative-surfaces}.



To compare these images, we divide both by the nonzero total target power
$m=\sum_{k,n}P_{k,n}=\max S_q$ and apply a pointwise feature transformation
$\phi$. For each direction pair, let $E_q(k,n)$ be the candidate-minus-target
feature error and $\ell_q$ its \gls{rms} over all bins and frames. For a selected
set of direction pairs $\mathcal Q$, the scalar loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal Q}$
averages these directional errors\footnote{Although a full mathematical
 equivalence is outside the scope of this work, we note that for 1D unit-mass
 distributions on a uniform grid, our \Glspl{cel} with a single accumulation direction
 and $\phi(u)=u$ yield the Cram\'er distance up to fixed grid and RMS
 normalisation. In turn, this relates \Glspl{cel} to the $1$-Wasserstein distance, which uses $L_1$ on Cumulative Distribution Functions, instead of Cram\'er's L2 \cite{szekely2013energy}\cite[Remark~2.30]{peyre2019computational}.};
$|\mathcal Q|$ is the number of pairs:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
E_q(k,n)&=\phi(\hat S_q(k,n)/m)-\phi(S_q(k,n)/m),\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal Q}&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal Q|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}\ell_q,
\qquad\ell_q=\operatorname{RMS}(E_q).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}


\section{Differentiable Plucked-String Musical Phrases}
\label{sec:renderer}
We use \Glspl{dwg} as a common and efficient choice for modelling plucked strings \cite{smith2010physical,depaula2026waveguides}. Our model has two oppositely travelling rails, each split at the excitation point into nut-side and bridge-side delays of $D_1$ and $D_2$ samples, respectively, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:waveguide}. For ideal delays and phase-inverting terminations, $f_0=f_s/[2(D_1+D_2)]$, where $f_s$ is the sample rate; the implemented model also compensates for interpolation and loss-filter phase. The relative pluck position arises as $D_2/(D_1+D_2)=0.23$ from the bridge. We synthesise two-second signals at $f_s=4$~kHz.

\begin{figure}[!b]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{\PaperRoot/figures/Waveguide.pdf}
\caption{Two-rail digital waveguide for plucked-string synthesis, with
nut-side delay $D_1$ and bridge-side delay $D_2$ on each rail.}
\label{fig:waveguide}
\end{figure}

Nut and bridge reflection gains are $-1$ and $-H_L(z)$, respectively, where
$H_L$ is the loss-filter transfer function, $z$ the transform variable, and
$z^{-1}$ a one-sample delay:
\begin{equation*}
H_L(z)=g\frac{1-a_1}{1-a_1z^{-1}},\qquad g=0.99,\quad a_1=0.2.
\end{equation*}
Here $g$ controls the DC gain and increasing $a_1$ increases high-frequency
attenuation, providing decay and damping controls. The resulting decay times
depend on $f_0$, since the loss filter is traversed once per round trip,
as also illustrated for a related model in
\cite[Fig.~3]{tablasdepaula2026karplus}. Output is the bridge-incident
travelling velocity.

Each new event excites a \gls{hrc},
\begin{equation*}
e_0(t)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{A_e}{2}[1-\cos(\pi t/T_e)],&0\leq t<T_e,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $e_0(t)$ is           amplitude at elapsed time $t$, $A_e=0.8$ its amplitude scale, and $T_e=10$~ms its duration. As a differentiable onset placement method, we use a Fourier phase-ramp delay for, using FLAMO~\cite{dalsanto2025flamo}. To place an onset by $\delta_o$ samples, we multiply the spectrum of $e_0$ by $e^{-j\omega\delta_o}$, where $\omega$ is angular frequency in radians per sample and $j^2=-1$. The excitation is zero-padded to an FFT length $2.048$ times the full signal length to reduce circular wrap-around~\cite[Ch.~7]{smith2007dft}. We constrain the DC and Nyquist bins to be real to preserve Hermitian symmetry~\cite[Ch.~7]{smith2007dft}. Shifted event spectra are summed before one inverse FFT, retaining the first two seconds as the summed excitation.

For gradient-based $f_0$ optimisation, we use real-valued delays with Thiran
all-pass interpolation.
These filters have unity magnitude and maximally flat group delay at
DC~\cite[p.~49]{laakso1996splitting}.
Each path combines an integer delay with an all-pass section. For an order-$M$
section, the approximated delay must exceed $M-1$ samples for
stability~\cite{laakso1996splitting}.
We use orders 1 and 3 on the bridge-side and nut-side paths, respectively:
at the upper end of our 80--320~Hz pitch range, the shorter path requires
less than two samples, precluding a stable order-3 section. We iteratively
adjust the delays to account for the all-pass and loss-filter phase at $f_0$.

Finally, we collapse the waveguide algebraically into a rational transfer
function and evaluate it in \gls{df2} as required for PhilTorch with its TorchLPC
backend for fast GPU backpropagation~\cite{yu2025philtorch,yu2024torchlpc}. For phrase synthesis, we reset
the recurrence history at each incoming event's onset, rounded down to a
sample boundary and detached from differentiation. Resetting smooths the
multi-pluck loss landscapes in our diagnostic; we hypothesise that removing
interference with residual string vibration contributes to this effect\footnote{Supplementary
loss landscapes at
\url{https://ptablasdpaula.github.io/ICASSP27-Phrase} document onset placement, delay interpolation choices and the state-reset
diagnostic and comparison of \gls{df2} and literal \gls{dwg} implementations.}.

\section{Experiments and Methodology}
\subsection{Loss Objectives}
\label{sec:loss-objectives}
We compare six base objectives. $L_1$ is mean absolute and $L_2$ mean
squared sample error. Wang et al. introduced multi-resolution spectral
comparison~\cite{wang2019nsf}; Engel et al. adopted it as \gls{mss} in
\gls{ddsp}~\cite{engel2020ddsp}, and Schw\"ar and M\"uller later revisited its
design~\cite{schwar2023mss}. Following the latter, \gls{mss} sums squared $L_2$
distances between
$\log(1+|\operatorname{STFT}|)$ magnitudes, where $|\cdot|$ is magnitude and
$\log$ the natural logarithm. Its prime-length flat-top windows
$\{17,31,67,127,257,509\}$ and hops $\{8,15,33,63,128,254\}$ limit leakage
without aligning \gls{dft} bins. Torres et al.'s \gls{sot}~\cite{torres2024sot}
combines frame-normalised \gls{fft}-512/hop-64 flat-top \gls{stft}-power transport with
their unbalanced quantile cutoff and $0.05$ Hann-windowed linear-magnitude \gls{mss}.
Fabiani et al.'s \gls{tfw}~\cite{fabiani2024jtfot} globally normalises a
periodic-Hann \gls{fft}-256/hop-128 magnitude \gls{stft} and projects it along ten angles.
We retain their scaling $\widetilde t=(10^3\,\mathrm{Hz/s})t$, making 1~s
commensurate with 1000~Hz in the ground cost. Immediately after this canonical
configuration, Experiment II tests a $1\,\mathrm{s}=1\,\mathrm{oct}$ variant
that changes \gls{tfw} coordinates to
$\log_2[\max(f,20)/20]$ octaves and seconds; $f$ is bin-centre Hz and the
20-Hz floor gives a finite origin. The sixth base objective is
Sec.~\ref{sec:losses}'s bidirectional variant, \gls{bicul}.
Experiment I compares the six base objectives. Experiment II compares the
original eight configurations and additionally reports Fade descriptively.

\subsection{CeL variants}
\label{sec:cel-variants}
Our base configuration averages all four direction pairs and uses
$\phi(u)=\sqrt{u}$. We test two separate modifications:

\emph{\acrshort{logq}} applies perceptually motivated frequency weighting.
For bin-centre frequency $f_k$, define $u_k=\log_2[\max(f_k,20)/20]$.
Let $w^f_{d_f,k}$ and $w^t_{d_t,n}$ denote adjacent interval widths in octaves
and seconds, following the accumulation directions. Replace the uniform RMS by
\begin{equation*}
\ell^{\rm LW}_q=\left[\frac{\sum_{k,n}
w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}E_q(k,n)^2}
{\sum_{k,n}w^f_{d_f,k}w^t_{d_t,n}}\right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation*}
Endpoints without a neighbouring interval have zero weight. Equal octave spans
receive equal total frequency weight; cumulative surfaces are unchanged.

\emph{Fade} makes accumulation more local. Within each direction's rectangle,
a contribution at absolute time and frequency separations $\Delta t$ and
$\Delta f$ from the receiving bin is multiplied by
$w(\Delta t;H_t)w(\Delta f;H_f)$, where
\begin{equation*}
w(d;H)=\max\!\left(0,1-\frac{\log(1+9d/H)}{\log 10}\right).
\end{equation*}
We fix $H_t=1$~s and $H_f=1000$~Hz throughout optimisation; each weight reaches
zero at its horizon. The same weights modify target and candidate accumulation.
Both variants retain the original total target power $m$, square-root features,
and four-direction average. Fade uses uniform RMS, without \acrshort{logq};
its logarithmic decay is distinct from octave-based error weighting.

\subsection{Experiment I: Single-event Gradient Diagnostic}
\label{sec:gradient-diagnostic}
\textcolor{red}{The single-event diagnostic and Fade recovery runs use 2.048x onset padding. The original eight phrase-recovery configurations still await reruns at this setting.}
We use a centred 160-Hz, 1.0-s target and a target-inclusive 25-by-25 lattice.
Time is linear over $\pm0.8$~s and pitch logarithmic over $\pm1$ octave, so
this relative lattice spans the complete registered candidate ranges
0.2--1.8~s and 80--320~Hz. Surfaces are independently min--max normalised.
Unit descent directions $-\nabla\mathcal L$ are displayed on an 11-by-11
sublattice with the target omitted, leaving 120 directions. A direction is
target-directed when its dot product with the target displacement is positive
in the normalised onset--pitch coordinates.

Figure~\ref{fig:landscapes} shows the six loss surfaces and directions using
Sec.~\ref{sec:renderer}'s fixed Fourier--Thiran renderer. Target and candidate
share this configuration, isolating loss geometry from renderer mismatch. This
is one full-range diagnostic case, not evidence of target invariance or a
population estimate.

\subsection{Experiment II: Phrase Recovery}
Seed 2027 generates 150 scrambled-Latin-hypercube targets at each of one, two,
four, six, and eight events. Pitches are log-mapped to 80--320~Hz; onsets span
0.2--1.8~s with at least 50~ms separation.

All candidate pitches start at 160~Hz and onsets at equal-cell centres over
0.2--1.8~s; independent logits bound coordinates to those same onset and pitch
ranges. Adam uses learning rate 0.05, default moments/epsilon, and no weight
decay. Initial-loss division conditions step scale only; reported values remain
raw. A relative raw-loss decrease of $10^{-4}$ resets patience. At patience 100
and 200, we restore the strict best, multiply the learning rate by 0.3 (floor
$10^{-5}$), and clear optimiser state; we stop at patience 250 or 3000 updates
and retain the best. Thus 150 targets $\times$ 8 losses $\times$ 5 event
counts${}={}$6000 historical fits. Fade adds 750 fits on the same targets and
optimisation schedule, using the updated 2.048 onset padding; Table~\ref{tab:pilot-compact}
reports it separately pending matched-padding baseline reruns.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\includegraphics[width=0.88\columnwidth]{\PaperRoot/figures/lsd_violin.pdf}

Let $i$ index an unordered prediction and $j$ a target. Their assignment cost is
\begin{equation*}
d_{ij}=\left[\frac{\log_2(\hat f_{0,i}/f_{0,j})}{2}\right]^2+
\left[\frac{\hat t_i-t_j}{1.6}\right]^2.
\end{equation*}
Hats mark predictions; $f_0$ is in Hz and $t$ in seconds; 2 octaves and 1.6~s
are the full ranges, making $d_{ij}$ dimensionless. The Hungarian
method~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian} finds the one-to-one pairing minimising
$\sum d_{ij}$. Table~\ref{tab:pilot-compact} reports its errors and
Fig.~\ref{fig:lsd} the audio-error distributions.

Across phrases, we report medians of within-phrase pitch \gls{mae} (cents) and onset
\gls{mae} (ms). Periodic-Hann \gls{fft}-256/hop-64 \gls{lsd} is objective-independent.

Fade is excluded from the following historical family comparison.
Post hoc, each phrase's \gls{bicul}-family score averages \gls{bicul} and \gls{logq}; its
\gls{tfw}-family score averages canonical \gls{tfw} and the $1\,\mathrm{s}=1\,\mathrm{oct}$
variant. For each count--metric cell, the paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank
test ranks the magnitudes of the 150 family-score differences and compares
positive with negative rank sums to assess whether the differences are centred
at zero~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. Five counts $\times$ three metrics give 15
$p$-values, jointly adjusted by Holm's procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Experiment I: Single-event Gradient Diagnostic}
In the loss order $L_1$, $L_2$, \gls{mss}, \gls{sot}, \gls{tfw}, and \gls{bicul}, the fixed
renderer gives 60.0\%, 49.2\%, 42.5\%, 51.7\%, 75.8\%, and 100.0\%
target-directed descent, respectively. Thus \gls{bicul} is the only objective with
120/120 target-directed directions in this registered renderer and diagnostic
case; Fig.~\ref{fig:landscapes} does not imply a renderer-independent ranking.

\subsection{Experiment II: Phrase Recovery}
Table~\ref{tab:pilot-compact} reports all nine configurations; Fig.~\ref{fig:lsd} shows the original eight
\gls{lsd} distributions. Among the original configurations, both \gls{bicul} variants are essentially exact at one and two
events. A \gls{bicul} variant has the lowest median pitch error at every count and
onset error through four; published linear-Hz \gls{tfw} has the lowest onset error
at six and eight.

Across these 15 tests, median paired family differences favour \gls{bicul}; 14
have $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\leq0.0069$, with only six-event onset above 0.05
($-10.5$~ms; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.186$).

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced \gls{bicul} for pluck-onset and pitch recovery. With fixed
Fourier--Thiran rendering, it gives 120/120 target-directed descents; reset
smooths the three-event \gls{df2} sweeps. Across 6000 fits, \gls{bicul} strengthens joint
recovery, with \gls{logq} improving descriptive results. At four events, \gls{logq}
\gls{bicul}'s median pitch error (19.1 cents) is
comparable to reported \glspl{jnd}~\cite{holmes2022pitch}, while its onset error
(6.75 ms) is below reported guitar-context timing \glspl{jnd}~\cite{camara2026timing};
from six events, both errors exceed those perceptual scales, with pitch error
above one semitone. This contextual comparison does not establish perceptual
equivalence between \gls{jnd} experiments and phrase-level mean absolute errors.

Future work should test reset generality, expressive techniques, higher sample
rates, and persistent state.
